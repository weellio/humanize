# Field lessons

Rules learned from real drafts that went out to real people. They sit on top of the main rules.

## Messages to real people (emails, DMs, replies)

- **Don't mirror their situation back to them.** "What I'm hearing is...", "It sounds like you...", "That
  kind of patience is hard-earned." They know their own situation. Reflecting it back reads as
  condescending, most of all to someone self-aware.
- **No sentiment filler.** "This is going to be awesome", "means the world", "I hope this finds you well",
  "I wanted to reach out". Cut it.
- **Promise only what the writer can deliver.** No claims about reach, skills or results the draft doesn't
  support. A short honest note beats a polished pitch.
- **It's fine to say it isn't a fit.** Pass kindly and leave the door open. Don't force a yes.
- **Match the recipient's register and energy.** A two-line message gets a short reply.
- **Lead with the outcome, in plain words.** Mention the tool or technology only when it helps the reader
  decide.

## Plain-language mode (emails, ads, scripts, instructions)

Use it when the reader needs to act:

- Present tense, active voice.
- No adverbs. They prop up weak verbs, so pick a stronger verb.
- Short sentences, one comma at most.
- Everyday words a 10-year-old knows (a grade 3 to 5 reading level).
- Say what to do instead of what to avoid. "Save first" beats "Don't forget to save."
- Cut repeated words, and any line you can remove without changing the meaning.

## Short video scripts (Shorts, Reels, TikTok)

Use HIDE:

- **Hook:** on-screen text that works with the sound off. A promise that doesn't give the answer away.
  Never the product or feature name.
- **Intrigue:** line one names the viewer's problem so they think "that's me". No instructions yet.
- **Delivery:** one idea. A second idea is a second video.
- **Exit:** one thing the viewer can do today.

Talk beside the viewer: "you", not "I built". Never "most people".

## News-style headlines and hooks

A named company or person, a strong verb and a real number beat an analytical summary. "Acme just cut 30% of
its sales team" beats "Acme's evolving go-to-market strategy". Use only facts from the source.

## Text inside apps and tools

Every line should help someone use the tool.

- **Keep:** labels, one short instruction beside a control that isn't obvious, errors that say what to do
  next, a safety note once per screen.
- **Cut:** narration of where the data came from, a "Source:" line under every section, repeated
  disclaimers, intros, developer notes, hedges.
- Put data credits in one "Data sources" place, and long explanations in the help page.

## Formatting for skimmers

Many readers skim. Some can't read a wall of text at all (dyslexia, a small phone screen).

- Short lines, one idea each.
- Bold the keyword at the start of a line.
- Tables for comparisons, numbered steps for processes.
- Blank lines between chunks. Long lists show the top few and "+N more".

## Quotes and dashes

- Quotes keep their original wording, em dashes and all. Only your own sentences follow these rules.
- No em dashes or en dashes in your own text. Ranges use a hyphen: 3-5.

## Start from real material

A rewrite of something a person said beats a draft from a blank prompt. Give the model notes, a transcript or
a voice memo. The model does setup and cleanup. The person supplies the opinions, stories and voice.
